Think of the 10-yard as the "garage cleanout" size. ## Which Greenbelt jobs suit this size

Greenbelt isn't a typical suburb, and that shapes which jobs the 10-yard handles best here. Old Greenbelt's core is a National Historic Landmark New Deal town — roughly 1,600 cooperative row houses owned by Greenbelt Homes, Inc. (GHI), built by the federal government starting in 1936-37, plus 1941-42 defense-era wood-frame units. These attached homes sit on crawlspaces and boiler rooms rather than full basements, which means renovations here tend to be interior and modest in scale: a bathroom redo, a weatherization or insulation job, an interior gut. GHI ran a multi-year Homes Improvement Program that added insulation, air sealing, and vapor barriers under the row houses, and that kind of focused work is a textbook fit for a 10-yard container.

The same goes for the estate downsizes and garage-style cleanouts that come up across the city's older housing. And in the newer pockets — the Ryan Homes townhomes at Greenbelt Station near the Metro and MARC station, or the condos and townhomes of Greenbelt East and Greenbriar — a finish-the-basement, flooring, or single-room project usually generates exactly the volume a 10-yard was built for. Greenbelt Station's units typically have narrow private driveways in front of one-car garages, and a 10-yard is the size that realistically fits there.

## Local logistics, permits, and placement

Here's where Greenbelt asks for a little more planning than the average town, and where it helps to work with a hauler who already knows the ground.

In **Old Greenbelt**, the historic design puts shared interior "garden side" courts on one side of each row of homes and pushes parking, trash areas, and services to the outer edge. The practical effect: a roll-off serving a court home often can't sit beside the unit at all. It has to stage in the shared exterior parking lot or court drive. On top of that, any improvement, alteration, or addition to a GHI home requires a GHI permit, and contractors are expected to have approved GHI permits in hand for the scope of work before starting. So before we drop a container, it's worth confirming your GHI approval and figuring out which shared lot or drive the truck can reach.

There's a **city layer**, too. The City of Greenbelt requires a permit for placing a container on a city street or right-of-way — and that includes most Old Greenbelt court parking. Driveways and HOA-approved staging spots typically don't need the city permit. In the 1970s-90s condo and townhome communities of Greenbelt East and Greenbriar, placement often means HOA approval and a shared lot rather than a private driveway, so it's the same idea wearing a different hat: confirm where the container can legally and physically sit before delivery day.

A quick note on addresses, since Greenbelt's ZIP codes trip people up. **20770** covers residential and business street addresses inside the city; **20768** is P.O. boxes only, so if your mail comes to a 20768 box, your actual jobsite is still a 20770 street address; and **20771** is reserved for NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center. When you call, give us the physical street address and we'll sort out the rest.
