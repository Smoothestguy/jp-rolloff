## What fits in a 15-yard dumpster

The 15-yard measures 16 ft long by 8 ft wide by 4 ft tall, and it holds roughly seven pickup-truck loads of debris. It comes with a 1.5-ton (3,000 lb) weight allowance. Around Rockville a 15-yard **includes delivery, pickup on request, and a 14-day rental with the 1.5-ton allowance built in — priced by material, so call for a quote**; extra days run $5/day, and any weight over the allowance is billed at $105 per additional ton.

The four-foot wall height is the quiet advantage of this size. The sides are low enough to pitch debris over the rail without a ramp or a fight, which matters a lot when you're carrying load after load out of a kitchen or hauling it up from a basement. But there's still real volume in there — enough to hold a full single-room job from start to finish. That's why this is the "single-room reno" size: the one to reach for when a 10-yard feels a touch tight but a 20 would leave you paying for air you'll never fill.

## Which Rockville jobs suit the 15-yard

The 15 lands right in the sweet spot for the one-room, one-system projects that Rockville's mid-century homes generate constantly:

- **Kitchen remodels.** Tearing out cabinets, countertops, and flooring from a single kitchen fills a 15-yard just about right — cabinets, drywall, old subfloor, and packaging, with a little room to spare.
- **Roofing tear-offs.** A single-layer asphalt tear-off on a typical Rockville rambler or colonial fits comfortably, with the 1.5-ton allowance covering most modest roofs. Shingles are heavy, so we'll talk through the square footage with you before we drop it.
- **Two-bath gut jobs.** Pulling tile, tubs, vanities, and old plumbing from a pair of bathrooms is a classic 15-yard load — more debris than a 10 wants, not enough to justify a 20.
- **Basement cleanouts.** Clearing a finished or half-finished basement — or hauling out the debris from finishing one — works well in the 15, since the lower walls make loading from a stairwell easier.

A neighborhood like Twinbrook (over near the 20851/20853 line) is almost a textbook case for this size. Twinbrook launched in 1946 on former farmland, and roughly 88% of its homes date to the 1940-to-1969 era — small WWII-era Cape Cods that were deliberately built with unfinished upper levels so young families could expand later. Decades on, those homes have seen second-story additions, dormers, and attic and basement finish-outs, and that kind of mid-size renovation throws off exactly the debris volume a 15-yard handles. If your project is bigger — a whole-home cleanout or a multi-room addition out in large-lot Derwood — that may be a 20 or a 30 instead, and we'll tell you so rather than upsell you.

## Local logistics: placement, permits, and drive time

Here's the thing about Rockville: it's really two cities in one when it comes to dumpster placement, and you have to ask "which Rockville?" before you can recommend a spot.

On one end you've got dense, alley-loaded and high-rise zones — King Farm, Rockville Town Square, and the Pike District along the North Bethesda border. King Farm is a 430-acre New Urbanist community built on a former dairy farm starting in 1997, and its townhomes sit on narrow lots with shared, alley-loaded garages and tight street frontage. That's placement that favors a smaller 10-to-15-yard container set carefully on the driveway or in the alley apron rather than a big roll-off. The Pike District side, transformed under the White Flint sector plans into a walkable urban core, leans heavily toward high-density multifamily where a single-family driveway often isn't even on the table.

On the other end you've got large-lot Derwood (ZIP 20855), a quieter, leafier stretch of bigger colonial homes — some on RE-1 acreage parcels near Rock Creek Regional Park — where long driveways make placement easy. And in between sit the tight Cape Cod streets of Twinbrook, where the homes are modest but the driveways usually take a 15 just fine. A 15-yard needs about 16 feet of straight length plus a little clearance and an open swing for the rear door, and we'll scout the spot with you so it fits the footprint you actually have. Boards go under the wheels on request to protect the surface.

**The Rockville permit rule.** The City of Rockville's default expectation is that dumpsters go on private property. A container sitting entirely on your driveway, lawn, or private property does **not** need a permit. A permit is only required to place the container in the public street or right-of-way — and that's a Public Works permit that takes about 2 to 3 business days, with the city granting it only after it's shown the container *can't* safely sit on private property (DPW at pw@rockvillemd.gov makes that call). So the driveway is almost always the easier and faster path, and it's what we recommend whenever the space allows.

One more Rockville wrinkle worth knowing: the city has three local historic districts — West End, East Rockville, and Lincoln Park (founded 1891, one of Maryland's first Black-owned home developments). If your home sits in one of those districts, exterior alterations can require a Historic Area Work Permit, with review scheduled inside roughly 45 days. That's a permit for the *work*, not the dumpster, but it's the kind of thing that affects your project timeline — so it's worth checking before you book the container.
