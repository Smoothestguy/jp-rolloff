## What fits in a 25-yard dumpster

This is the "major construction" size — the second-largest container most homeowners and contractors ever need, and the extra capacity earns its keep on dense, multi-room jobs. The 25-yard holds about 7.5 standard pickup loads, which is the difference between finishing a big build in a single haul and pausing the schedule for a second container.

A few honest words on weight. The 2.5-ton allowance is generous for mixed construction debris — framing lumber, drywall, old cabinetry, flooring, trim, packaging, and general demolition. But dense materials add up fast. A full layer of asphalt shingles, plaster and lath, tile, or masonry can hit the tonnage cap well before the box looks full, so if your load is heavy rather than bulky, tell us up front and we will steer you to the right plan. For voluminous-but-light debris — a gutted kitchen, a whole floor torn back to the studs, the contents of a full remodel — the 25-yard's footprint is exactly where it shines.

Because this is a true major-construction size, measure twice before booking. For longer driveways and bigger builds the 25 is ideal, but if you are doing a single bathroom or a modest declutter, a smaller box will save you money. When in doubt, call and describe the job — we would rather right-size it than oversell you.

## Which College Park jobs suit the 25-yard

College Park is defined by the University of Maryland, and that shapes the kind of heavy work that lands here. The housing stock skews old and varied — most homes were built between roughly 1940 and 1969, with Old Town and Berwyn holding houses over a century old. Old Town College Park alone is a historic district of nearly 300 buildings, from early-1900s Victorians and Queen Annes to Colonial Revivals and Arts & Crafts bungalows. When owners take one of these older frame houses all the way — a rear or second-story addition, a full gut-remodel, a roof and structure stripped back and rebuilt — the debris volume jumps into 25-yard territory.

A **home addition** on an Old Town or Calvert Hills lot generates framing offcuts, old siding, excavated interior finishes, and packaging in real volume. A **whole-house remodel** of one of Berwyn's two-story wood-frame homes or a Calvert Hills Craftsman produces a steady stream of plaster, lath, cabinetry, flooring, and trim that a smaller box simply cannot stay ahead of. A **large roofing** tear-off on a pre-WWII frame house is a classic 25-yard job — just keep the tonnage in mind, since old shingles and built-up layers are heavy. And for a **commercial cleanout** along the Route 1 / Baltimore Avenue development strip, the 25 gives crews room to clear a space fast.

That Route 1 corridor matters here. The "Greater College Park" redevelopment wave is a multi-billion-dollar push, with student-oriented and affordable apartment projects rising where motels and strip-mall sites once stood between the campus and the neighborhoods. Sustained construction and demolition like that feeds steady demand for larger 25- and 30-yard roll-offs full of heavy construction debris. Our commercial accounts get net-15 terms, which keeps the paperwork simple when several jobs are running at once.

The other rhythm unique to College Park is the academic calendar. Most off-campus student houses and apartments run August-to-July leases, so late summer brings a wave of move-out cleanouts and landlord turnover work in the campus corridor and around Calvert Hills, where many older homes have been converted into multi-unit rentals. A heavy turnover — a landlord gutting and rebuilding a converted house between tenants — can absolutely justify a 25-yard. A routine furniture-and-junk move-out usually does not, and we will tell you so.

## Permits, placement, and drive time

Here is the part College Park property owners want to know. The City of College Park requires a right-of-way permit when the dumpster sits in a city street — that permit is issued through Public Works. College Park's residential placement rules can be stricter than most towns, though: depending on the current city code, a permit may apply even on a private driveway, not only the street. So rather than guess, we confirm the exact requirement for your address with the city before we deliver — around campus that question matters more than in most suburbs.

That is because placement in College Park is genuinely tight. Old Town was platted on long, narrow lots — a standard parcel runs about 50 by 200 feet — so driveways tend to be single-car-width and homes sit close to their neighbors. A 20-foot box is long, and on a lot like that the truck may need to plan careful backing or a driveway-end drop-off. Near the UMD campus and in North College Park, many homes have few or no driveways and rely on street parking, and several of those crowded blocks are already permit-only for parking. Where a driveway cannot fit the roll-off, street placement — and the associated City right-of-way permit — becomes the practical option. The smartest move is a quick measure before delivery: send us a photo or a short description of the spot and we will confirm the fit, flag whether you will need the street permit, and plan the approach before we roll. You want a flat, firm surface with clear overhead access — no low branches or wires where the truck lifts the container.

From our Beltsville dispatch, College Park is a quick run straight down Route 1, so a same-week drop is rarely a problem. Being close means we can get out to look at a tricky placement before the project starts rather than discovering it on delivery day — useful on the narrow historic lots near the airport and the campus, where the right staging plan saves everyone a headache.
