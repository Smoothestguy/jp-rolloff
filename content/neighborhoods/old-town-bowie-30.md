## What the 30-yard holds

The 30-yard measures roughly 22 feet long, 8 feet wide, and 6 feet tall. That long, tall box swallows about nine pickup-truck loads of material, which is why it is the go-to for jobs that throw off a steady wall of debris rather than a few bags. Pricing is per material: a 30-yard for construction debris starts at $665, with other materials priced separately (aggregates such as concrete, brick, and dirt are only offered in the 10-, 15-, and 20-yard sizes). That price includes delivery, a 14-day rental, and a 3-ton (6,000 lb) weight allowance; pickup is on request, so call to schedule it when you are ready. Keep the box past 14 days and it is +$5 per day, and any weight over the 3-ton allowance is billed at $105 per additional ton.

One honest word on that weight allowance. The 30-yard's volume fills up fastest with bulky, lighter material — framing lumber, old siding and lath, drywall and plaster, worn cabinetry, roofing tear-off, and general construction debris. If your load leans heavy with concrete, brick, dirt, or tile, the cube fills slowly but the tonnage climbs fast, and a smaller bin or a dedicated heavy-debris container is usually the smarter call. When you phone in, tell us what is going in and we will steer you to the right size before the truck rolls.

## Why Old Town Bowie jobs reach this size

Old Town Bowie is the original Bowie. Platted in 1870 as "Huntington City" by developer Benjamin Plumb, it was laid out on a rectilinear grid around the Baltimore & Potomac Railroad junction, with tree-named streets running one way and numbered streets crossing them. The Bowie Railroad Buildings at 8614 Chestnut Avenue — the restored station that served passenger trains from 1872 until 1989 and joined the National Register in 1998 — still anchor the district. This compact 19th-century core predates the 1960s Levitt subdivisions that make up the rest of Bowie by roughly ninety years, and that history is exactly why projects here so often need the largest box we carry.

The housing stock is predominantly wood-frame, much of it dating from around 1880 onward — Victorian-era I-houses, front-gabled and L-shaped plans, American Foursquares, bungalows, and Cape Cods, with Queen Anne, Colonial Revival, and Dutch Colonial Revival detailing. The MNCPPC survey of the district documents roughly 590 primary buildings, many well over a century old, packed into a small footprint. Century-old frame homes generate a different kind of debris than a standardized tract house, and the heavy work tends to reach 30-yard volume:

- **Full demolition.** Gutting an 1880s-era frame house down to the studs — pulling old plaster and lath, dropped ceilings, layered flooring, knob-and-tube-era walls, and long-ago additions — produces far more material than owners expect. The 30-yard keeps a demo crew moving without stopping to swap containers mid-job.
- **Commercial site work.** Old Town's 9th Street spine and its civic buildings, plus the city-backed revitalization funded in 2024 that plans galleries, a brewery, and a performing arts venue, mean tenant turnovers and buildouts that throw off a continuous stream of fixtures, flooring, and ceiling material. One large box keeps the dumpster off the critical path.
- **Bulk waste hauls.** Full estate and whole-house clear-outs of homes that have stayed in one family for generations pile up fast. Nine truck-loads of capacity means you load once and we haul once.
- **Multi-family cleanouts.** Turning over several units of older frame housing at once — appliances, carpet, mattresses, and accumulated debris together — is exactly the volume the 30-yard was built to handle.

For a single-room remodel or a routine cleanout in Old Town, a smaller 10- or 15-yard can is almost always the better fit on these lots. The 30-yard shines when capacity, not budget, is the thing that would otherwise cost you a second trip.

## Placement on Old Town's tight lots and narrow streets

This is where Old Town Bowie asks for more thought than newer parts of the city. The 1870 grid was built around small town lots and close-set frame houses clustered tightly near the station — the polar opposite of the wide, uniform yards and long driveways of the Levitt sections. Streets here are narrow, driveways are short, and curbside room is limited compared with Bowie's suburban subdivisions. A 30-yard is a big box on a long frame, and the driver needs a clear, straight run-up to back the trailer in. On many Old Town lots that run-up is the deciding factor, so it is worth a real look before we arrive.

That makes the placement conversation matter more here than anywhere else in Bowie. The City of Bowie requires a right-of-way permit only if the dumpster sits in a city street — driveways and HOA-approved staging do not. Given Old Town's short driveways and tight grid, the public street is more often the only workable spot, so a permit comes up more frequently than it would out in the wider sections. The city's Public Works Department handles that right-of-way review, and we are glad to walk you through it before your container shows up. Tell us your exact street and where you picture the box, and we will plan the drop around the narrow approach rather than discovering the problem on delivery day.

One more note specific to this district: Old Town Bowie falls under a Development District Overlay Zone administered through Prince George's County and M-NCPPC, which can apply added design review to projects that alter older structures. That is between you and your contractor or the county, not something the dumpster touches — but if your job is permitted and scheduled around that review, just let us know the timeline and we will fit the container to it.
