## Why the 10-yard fits Overbrook

Overbrook is one of the "O sections" of Levitt and Sons' Belair at Bowie, and locals just call it the O section. The street-naming convention that runs through Belair holds here too — every street in the section begins with the letter O, like Overbrook Lane — which honestly makes our drivers' lives easier. We can confirm an address before the truck ever leaves the yard.

It's a small community, roughly seven blocks tucked into the northeast corner of Bowie, with easy access to U.S. Route 50, Samuel Ogle Park, and the WB&A Trail. The housing stock is classic mid-1960s Levitt: Cape Cods, split-levels, and brick ranch-style homes set along narrow residential streets with mature trees shading the lawns. Overbrook was one of the last Belair sections of detached single-family homes built, realized between roughly 1964 and 1968, so most houses here are now 55 to 60 years old.

That age and that street layout are the whole story when it comes to picking a dumpster size. The streets are narrow, and a big container parked on the roadway gets in everyone's way fast. The 10-yard's footprint is about 12 feet long, 8 feet wide, and 3.5 feet tall — small enough to fit a single car spot. On a typical Overbrook lot, that means it stages cleanly on the home's own driveway instead of out on the road.

That's the real advantage of the smallest box here. You get a container sized to a one-room job without paying for cubic yards you'll never fill, and it fits where you actually have room to put it.

## Driveway placement keeps you out of the permit

Levitt built these Belair homes with paved driveways, so nearly every house in Overbrook already has a hard surface ready for a dumpster — no need to touch the street at all. That matters more than it sounds, because of how Bowie handles permits.

The City of Bowie only requires a right-of-way permit when a dumpster sits in a city street. If we set the 10-yard on your driveway — which is what we recommend on these narrow O-section streets anyway — you're not in the right-of-way, and the permit question simply never comes up. Your street stays clear, your neighbors stay happy, and your project moves faster.

If your particular situation forces street placement — a short driveway, a tight turn under the trees, a shared parking arrangement — that's when the city's rules kick in, and a homeowner would coordinate with Bowie's public works and permitting office before the box goes down. The honest truth, though, is that on a standard Overbrook driveway we rarely need to go there. We'll walk through it with you on the phone before we roll a truck.
