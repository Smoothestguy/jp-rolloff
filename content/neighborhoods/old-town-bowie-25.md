Old Town Bowie is not the Bowie most people picture. While the rest of the city is filled with the wide streets and uniform tract homes that the Levitt company built in the 1960s, Old Town Bowie is the original — a rectilinear grid platted back in 1870 by developer Benjamin Plumb around the Baltimore & Potomac Railroad junction, with tree-named streets like Chestnut Avenue running one way and numbered streets crossing them. It started life as "Huntington City," a cluster of small town lots, shops, a hotel, and churches packed tightly around the train station. That history is exactly why a 25-yard dumpster here takes a little more planning than it would out in the newer subdivisions, and why it pays to work with a hauler who already knows the district. We're JP Roll-Off Service, a family-owned company dispatched out of Beltsville, and we run these streets often.

A 25-yard container is our "major construction" size, and in Old Town Bowie it earns its keep on the big jobs. The neighborhood's housing stock is predominantly wood-frame and dates from roughly 1880 onward — Victorian-era I-houses, front-gabled and L-shaped plans, American Foursquares, bungalows, and Cape Cods, often dressed up with Queen Anne, Colonial Revival, or Dutch Colonial Revival detailing. The MNCPPC historic survey documents around 590 primary buildings here, ranging in age from about 1880 to the present, all crowded into a small footprint. When one of those century-old frame homes gets a full gut-rehab, a whole-house remodel, a sizable roofing tear-off, or a true addition, the debris adds up fast — and that's where the 25-yard earns its spot in the driveway.

## Tight lots and narrow streets: placement here matters

Here's the part that separates Old Town Bowie from the rest of the city. This is a 19th-century grid of small town lots and narrow streets, and that original Huntington layout never anticipated an 18-foot steel box. Driveways tend to be short, curbside room is limited, and the historic blocks around 9th Street — where the 1939 firehouse and the largest concentration of historic buildings sit — don't have the generous frontage you'd find in a Levitt cul-de-sac. A 25-yard is a tall, sizable container, and on some lots it simply won't fit where a smaller can would.

That's not a reason to avoid the size — it's a reason to plan the drop. When you call, tell us where you're thinking of staging it: the driveway, a side yard, or the street. We'll talk through the angles, the clearance, and whether the box can be set without blocking your neighbor or pinning in a parked car. Because these lots are tight, a few minutes on the phone beforehand saves a lot of headache on delivery day. We deliver with boards under the container to protect older driveways and aprons, which matters more on the surfaces around here than on fresh suburban concrete.

## Permits and the historic overlay

Two things to keep in mind in Old Town Bowie. First, the City of Bowie requires a right-of-way permit only if the dumpster sits in a city street. If we set it on your driveway or on HOA-approved staging, no street permit is needed. Given how narrow the streets are in the historic grid, keeping the container on private property is usually the smoother path — but if the street is your only option, get the permit lined up before we deliver.

Second, Old Town Bowie falls under a Development District Overlay Zone administered through Prince George's County and M-NCPPC planning, which applies tailored design-review standards to the historic district. That overlay governs the work you do to the structure itself, not the dumpster — but if you're altering one of these older homes or commercial buildings, it's worth confirming your project's review requirements before the demolition starts so your timeline lines up with the haul. We're not your permit office, but we've staged enough jobs here to flag when something's worth double-checking.

It's also worth noting the district is on the upswing. A city-backed revitalization of Old Town Bowie, funded in 2024 with construction expected to begin in 2025, plans to bring in art galleries, a brewery, and a performing arts venue. That kind of tenant-improvement and commercial-buildout work is exactly the sort of cleanout a 25-yard handles well.
