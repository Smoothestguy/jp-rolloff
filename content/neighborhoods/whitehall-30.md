## What the 30-yard holds

The 30-yard measures roughly 22 feet long, 8 feet wide, and 6 feet tall. That long, tall box swallows about fourteen pickup-truck loads of material, which is why it is the go-to for jobs that throw off a steady wall of debris rather than a few bags. Pricing is per material — for construction debris the 30-yard is priced by what you are throwing away, with other materials quoted separately (aggregates like concrete, brick, dirt, and tile are only available in the 10, 15, and 20-yard sizes), so call for a quote. Every drop includes delivery, pickup on request, and a 14-day rental with a 3-ton weight allowance; extra days are $5 each, and weight beyond the allowance is billed at $105 per additional ton. Pickup is on request, so give us a call to schedule the haul-away.

One honest word on that weight allowance. The 30-yard's volume fills up fastest with bulky, lighter material — framing lumber, drywall, old cabinetry, roofing tear-off, lath and plaster, furniture, and general construction debris. If your load leans heavy with concrete, brick, dirt, or tile, the cube fills slowly but the tonnage climbs fast, and a smaller bin or a dedicated heavy-debris container is usually the smarter call. When you phone in, tell us what is going in and we will steer you to the right size before the truck rolls.

## Placement on Whitehall's Levitt lots and golf-course backs

Here is the one piece worth thinking through before you book a 30-yard in Whitehall. Levitt built six models across Belair — two Cape Cod variations, two Ranchers, the Colonial, the Country Clubber, and the Manor House — and Whitehall's homes range from compact Capes around 1,400 square feet up to the larger Country Clubber near 2,400. That mix matters for placement as much as for debris volume: the smaller-Cape streets sit on tighter lots with less driveway room, while the larger homes generally give a driver more to work with. The 30-yard is the longest box we run and it needs a clear, straight run-up so the trailer can back in without clipping a neighbor's curb cut or a mature tree. On most Whitehall driveways that works fine, but the tighter lots are exactly where getting the placement right matters most, because there is not much spare room to correct on the fly.

There is a second wrinkle unique to this section. Whitehall directly borders the public 18-hole Bowie Golf Club, and lots that back onto the course can complicate where debris gets staged near the rear property line. If your project is feeding the dumpster from the back of the house — a common setup on a full demo or a big cleanout — it pays to think through the path from the work area to the bin before the truck arrives, especially on a course-backing lot where the rear boundary is not yours to stage across.

That placement decision ties directly to the permit question, and in Whitehall it usually has an easy answer. The City of Bowie requires a right-of-way permit only if the dumpster sits in a city street — driveways and HOA-approved staging do not. Whitehall is an established, non-gated Levitt section organized around a civic association rather than a deed-restricted, dues-collecting HOA, so in practice, if we can fit the 30-yard on your own driveway, you almost certainly need no permit and no sign-off at all. If a local association does have its own staging rules, those are best checked with them directly. And if your only workable spot turns out to be the public street, the city's right-of-way review applies, and we are glad to walk you through what is involved before your container shows up.
