## The Belair Renovation Wave Is Here

If you live in Belair, your house has a twin. Probably a dozen of them on your own street. When William Levitt bought the 2,280-acre Belair estate in 1957 and opened home sales on October 8, 1960, his crews finished six to ten houses a day, assembly-line style, through 1964. The result was roughly 7,000 near-identical Cape Cods and Ranchers — plus the Colonial, Country Clubber, and Manor House models — sitting on modest ~8,400-square-foot lots across the S, B, K, F, and T sections (Somerset, Buckingham, Kenilworth, Foxhill, and Tulip Grove).

Those homes are now 60 to 65 years old. And they're all hitting the same milestones at the same time: first-ever roof tear-offs, kitchen and bathroom gut-remodels, and the estate cleanouts that come as the original-owner generation moves on. ## Why the 20-Yard Is the Right Size for a Belair Home

A 20-yard roll-off measures about **16 feet long by 8 feet wide by 5 feet tall**. It swallows roughly **six pickup-truck loads** of debris and comes with a **2-ton (4,000-pound) weight allowance** — enough capacity for a real project without paying for a 30-yard you can't fill.

Here's the Belair-specific twist: these homes were built on **concrete slabs, not basements**. That single design choice changes how a renovation works. There's no basement to haul out, and demolition debris is generated right at grade — drywall, cabinets, flooring, and fixtures come straight out a side door and into the can at ground level. You're not lugging anything up a flight of stairs, which makes loading a driveway dumpster fast and clean.

Because the housing stock is so uniform, the jobs repeat too. Whole streets share the same roof size, the same floor plan, and the same remodel needs. The 20-yard handles the bread-and-butter Belair projects:

Belair was laid out in the classic Levittown style — tree-lined, curving residential streets with mature canopy and modest curb frontage. Combine that with the standardized ~8,400-square-foot lots and the original Levitt-built driveways, and the practical answer is almost always the same: **the dumpster goes on your driveway.**

That's good news. Those driveways were poured to a consistent size across the sections, and a 20-yard can sits comfortably on most of them while leaving you room to work. We'll set wood down under the wheels and rollers to protect the surface, and we'll position the door end where your debris flow makes sense. The narrower curb frontage and big street trees common throughout the F and T sections are exactly why we keep cans off the street whenever we can — it's cleaner, safer, and avoids paperwork.

### The Bowie Permit Rule, Plain and Simple

Here's the rule for the City of Bowie: a dumpster placed on **private property — your driveway — generally does not require a city permit.** A permit comes into play only when the can has to sit in the **public street or right-of-way**, where it can't obstruct traffic. Because Belair's driveways handle a 20-yard with room to spare, the overwhelming majority of our Belair drops need **no permit at all.**

If your particular spot forces a street placement, we'll flag it early and walk you through the right-of-way permit step so there are no surprises. And if your section carries any HOA bylaws on placement or duration, check those before scheduling — Bowie's original Levitt sections tend to be lighter on covenants than newer communities, but it's always worth a quick confirmation.
